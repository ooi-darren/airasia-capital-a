# 01: History and Origins

**Business question**: Before diagnosing whether AirAsia's September 2026 crisis is a genuine solvency risk or an overreaction, it helps to understand how AirAsia and its long-haul affiliate AirAsia X got here. Both the RM1 buyout that built the group and the near-death experiences it survived along the way shape how seriously to take today's numbers.

--- CELL id=31b1e3fb ---
## From a government-linked carrier to a RM1 buyout

AirAsia was founded in **1993** as a government-linked domestic carrier, years before Tony Fernandes was ever involved. **[PUBLIC]**

In **December 2001**, Tony Fernandes and Kamarudin Meranun, through their company Tune Air Sdn Bhd, acquired the loss-making airline for a nominal **RM1**, taking on approximately **RM40 million** in existing debt. This is the founding story the company itself tells, and it is corroborated across independent sources. **[PUBLIC]**

The tagline "Now Everyone Can Fly" originates from this 2001 relaunch, not from AirAsia X specifically, reflecting Fernandes's stated vision of making air travel affordable and accessible in a region where flying had been a premium product. **[PUBLIC]**

--- CELL id=9bccc8a3 ---
## The long-haul affiliate: AirAsia X

AirAsia X was established in **2006** as FlyAsianXpress Sdn Bhd, rebranded AirAsia X in **2007**, and flew its first international route from Kuala Lumpur to Gold Coast, Australia in **November 2007**. It was set up as a separate legal entity from AirAsia Berhad specifically to run long-haul, low-cost routes, a structural choice that matters later: it is this same AirAsia X entity that ends up as the legal acquirer in the January 2026 reverse acquisition (Notebook 3). **[PUBLIC]**

--- CELL id=fad55d53 ---
## Who is actually the mother company here

This is worth being precise about, since the group's later restructuring inverts it. **AirAsia Group Berhad, the 1993 company Tony Fernandes bought for RM1, is the original parent.** It later renamed itself **Capital A Berhad in January 2022** (Notebook 2), but it is the same continuous legal entity: today's Capital A is that original mother company, just under a different name. **AirAsia X Berhad was never a subsidiary of it**; it was founded independently in 2006/2007 as its own separately listed sister company. **[PUBLIC]**

That ownership picture flips in 2026. AirAsia X legally acquires the group's short/medium-haul airlines from Capital A (Notebook 3), becomes the parent of the combined aviation business, and then renames *itself* "AirAsia Group Berhad" in July 2026, reusing the original parent's old name for a legally different company. From that point on, "Capital A" (the true original mother) and "AirAsia Group" (now legally the former sister company) are two separate listed businesses linked by a minority cross-shareholding, not one still sitting inside the other (Notebook 4 maps this fully).

--- CELL id=fb817249 ---
## Timeline

--- CELL id=99ce7239 ---
Notice the two entities' paths converge only in 2026. AirAsia (the short/medium-haul airline, later folded into Capital A) and AirAsia X (the long-haul affiliate) each built their own separate history, including separately triggering PN17 distressed-issuer status around COVID, covered in full in Notebook 2. The 2026 reverse acquisition was not two strangers merging; it was two related entities with three decades of shared and separate history finally combining under one roof.

--- CELL id=86db47e5 ---
## Explain It Simply

AirAsia was not always Tony Fernandes's company. It started as a small, loss-making, government-linked airline in 1993. In 2001, Fernandes and his business partner bought it for just RM1, took on its debts, and turned it into the low-cost airline that made "Now Everyone Can Fly" a household phrase. That original company later renamed itself Capital A. A separate long-haul sister airline, AirAsia X, was created in 2006 to fly longer international routes under the same brand but as its own legal company, never owned by the original parent. For most of their history these two airlines ran side by side, not as one company. Then in 2026, the relationship flipped: AirAsia X bought the original company's airlines and took over the "AirAsia Group" name for itself, while the original company (now Capital A) kept everything else and became a minority shareholder in the very airline group it used to own outright.

--- CELL id=5b180bdf ---
## Glossary

- **Tune Air Sdn Bhd**: the private investment vehicle Tony Fernandes and Kamarudin Meranun used to acquire AirAsia in 2001.
- **AirAsia X**: the long-haul, low-cost affiliate founded in 2006/2007, legally separate from AirAsia Berhad for most of its history.
- **PN17**: Bursa Malaysia's Practice Note 17, a classification for financially distressed listed companies, covered in depth in Notebook 2.

--- CELL id=b2316aa6 ---
## Why Is This Happening?

**Why did AirAsia and AirAsia X stay separate legal entities for two decades instead of merging earlier?** Splitting short/medium-haul (AirAsia) from long-haul (AirAsia X) let each business raise its own financing and bear its own risk, long-haul routes have very different aircraft, fuel, and crew economics from short-haul ones, so keeping them in separate listed entities let investors judge each on its own merits rather than one business's troubles dragging down the other's share price. That logic held for two decades. The 2026 merger reversed it, betting that a single combined entity is now more efficient than two separately financed ones, a bet this project tests directly in Notebook 3.

# 02: From COVID Collapse to the Twin PN17 Sagas (2020-2026)

**Business question**: Is the September 2026 crisis AirAsia's first real brush with insolvency, or does the group have real, recent precedent for surviving something this serious, and for changing what it even is along the way? The answer changes how much weight to put on today's numbers versus the panic around them.

--- CELL id=a540f16f ---
## The COVID-19 collapse: where this story actually starts

Before either PN17 classification, before any restructuring plan, there was an operational collapse severe enough to make both inevitable. When Malaysia's borders closed and global air travel stopped in March 2020, AirAsia's fleet was grounded almost in its entirety, essentially overnight, with no revenue to replace it.

The scale of the drop, comparing the same quarter one year apart: group revenue fell from **RM2.31 billion in Q1 FY2020** to **RM298.22 million in Q1 FY2021**, an **87% year-on-year fall**. The group posted a **net loss of RM767.42 million for that single quarter (Q1 FY2021)** alone. Across the full year, FY2020 revenue fell to **RM3.1 billion from RM11.86 billion**, and the group's net loss after tax was **RM5.9 billion for the year**. **[PUBLIC]**

This was not a paper loss. By October 2020, AirAsia and AirAsia X together confirmed the retrenchment of roughly **10% of their combined 24,000-strong workforce, about 2,400 employees**, after an earlier June 2020 round had already cut 111 cabin crew, 172 pilots, and 50 engineers. **[PUBLIC]**

--- CELL id=d76711a9 ---
This is the collapse that both PN17 classifications below are a direct consequence of, not an unrelated prior chapter. Every subsequent event in this notebook, the debt restructuring, the court schemes, the eventual merger, exists because of the hole this quarter (and the several quarters like it that followed) blew in both entities' balance sheets.

--- CELL id=a93d684a ---
## Two entities, two separate distress classifications

PN17 (Practice Note 17) is Bursa Malaysia's classification for financially distressed listed companies, triggered when core financial ratios (commonly, shareholders' equity falling below 25% of share capital) breach set thresholds. Both AirAsia Group (the old parent, later renamed Capital A) and AirAsia X Berhad (the long-haul affiliate) triggered PN17 criteria around **30 July 2020**, amid COVID-driven travel collapse. Bursa's relief measures (April 2020 to June 2021) delayed formal classification for both. **[PUBLIC]**

From there, the two entities' paths diverged and were resolved separately, not as one combined process.

--- CELL id=8772f269 ---
## AirAsia X's saga: the harsher one

AirAsia X was formally classified PN17 in **November 2021**, after its auditor issued a disclaimer of opinion on its accounts. By then, it had already proposed a **RM63.6 billion debt restructuring** (October 2020). Creditors approved the plan in November 2021 (100% of Class A and C creditors, 97.6% of Class B), and the High Court sanctioned it in December 2021. Under the approved scheme, creditors were paid only **0.5% of the RM33.65 billion** they were owed, a severe haircut that wiped the slate clean. AirAsia X formally exited PN17 status on **21 November 2023**. **[PUBLIC]**

--- CELL id=8866ba86 ---
## Capital A's saga: resolved through the merger itself

Capital A was formally classified PN17 in **January 2022**. Its own regularisation plan was not completed independently; it was completed **through** the January 2026 reverse acquisition itself: disposing its aviation businesses to the now debt-clean AirAsia X, distributing and listing AAX shares to entitled shareholders, and a High Court-approved capital reduction of approximately **RM5.5 billion**. Capital A formally exited PN17 on **20 May 2026**, closing a six-year restructuring chapter. **[PUBLIC]**

--- CELL id=d8d37cb2 ---
## Not just a restructuring: becoming Capital A

While the PN17 process ground on, the parent company also changed what it was. On **28 January 2022**, AirAsia Group Berhad renamed itself **Capital A Berhad**. This was not a cosmetic rebrand timed to a restructuring milestone; it was a public statement of strategic direction. Group CEO Tony Fernandes framed it directly: **"today's announcement reinforces we are not just an airline anymore."** **[PUBLIC]**

The rationale was a shift in the group's revenue mix, targeting roughly **50% of revenue from non-airline businesses by 2026**, up from the airline-dominated mix the group had run on for two decades. The businesses meant to carry that shift were already inside the group: **BigPay** (digital wallet and fintech, which secured up to **USD100 million in financing from South Korea's SK Group** in August 2021) and the **AirAsia super-app**, which had reached **10.6 million average monthly active users by Q2 2022** (up 236% year-on-year), aggregating flights, food delivery, and other services under one platform. The AirAsia brand itself was kept for the airlines; only the group holding company was renamed. **[PUBLIC]**

Read alongside the PN17 timeline below, the sequence is not coincidental: a company mid-restructuring, legally required to prove Bursa it had a credible path back to financial health, was simultaneously trying to convince investors that its future value lay outside the very airline business that had just needed to be rescued. Both things were true and were happening at once.

--- CELL id=c6f5fc62 ---
## Timeline

--- CELL id=d6e50e5a ---
The sequence matters: AirAsia X had to become clean on its **own** balance sheet first (by November 2023) before it was in any position to serve as the vehicle that absorbed Capital A's aviation assets and completed Capital A's own regularisation. This was not two unrelated companies merging for growth; it was a debt-scarred subsidiary graduating into the instrument that finished fixing its own former parent.

--- CELL id=b3bcb44a ---
## What this means for reading the September 2026 crisis

The group has direct, recent precedent for surviving something more severe than a bad quarter: a court-sanctioned restructuring that paid ordinary creditors half a cent on the ringgit, and it still exists, flies, and completed a six-year turnaround. That does not mean the current crisis is safe to dismiss; the working-capital deficit identified in Notebook 14 is real. It does mean this is not the group's first real test, and the institutional playbook (debt restructuring, court-sanctioned schemes, Bursa PN17 processes) has been used successfully before.

--- CELL id=8cc61487 ---
## Explain It Simply

COVID hit AirAsia harder than almost anything a normal business plan accounts for: revenue dropped 87% in a single year, the company lost hundreds of millions of ringgit in one quarter alone, and about 1 in 10 employees lost their jobs. That collapse is why both AirAsia's short-haul parent company and its long-haul sister airline, AirAsia X, were declared financially distressed by the stock exchange around the same time. But they were fixed separately. AirAsia X went through a brutal debt restructuring where the people it owed money to got paid less than one cent for every ringgit owed, and it took until late 2023 to come out the other side clean. In the middle of all this, the parent company also renamed itself Capital A and started telling investors its future was about more than flying planes, pointing to its digital wallet, cargo business, and super-app instead. Only after AirAsia X was clean could it be used, in January 2026, to absorb the parent company's airline business and finish cleaning up the parent's own books too. So this group has already been through a genuine near-death experience once, five years ago, survived it, and changed what it calls itself along the way. That's useful context for how seriously to take the current crisis.

--- CELL id=45218257 ---
## Glossary

- **PN17 (Practice Note 17)**: Bursa Malaysia's classification for listed companies in financial distress, typically triggered when shareholders' equity falls below a set threshold of share capital.
- **Regularisation plan**: the formal turnaround plan a PN17 company must submit and execute to be uplifted from distressed status.
- **Scheme creditors**: creditors bound by a court-sanctioned debt restructuring scheme, even those who did not individually vote for it, once the required majority approves.
- **Non-airline revenue**: revenue from businesses other than flying passengers, such as BigPay, Teleport, and the AirAsia super-app; the basis of Capital A's 2022 rebrand rationale.

--- CELL id=9596f212 ---
## Why Is This Happening?

**Why would Capital A choose to resolve its own PN17 status through a merger rather than its own separate restructuring?** By 2025, AirAsia X was the cleaner, more attractive vehicle: a listed entity with no legacy debt overhang, precisely because of its own brutal 2021-2023 restructuring. Using it to absorb Capital A's aviation assets let Capital A satisfy Bursa's regularisation requirements while consolidating the group's flying operations into a single, better-capitalised entity, solving two problems (Capital A's PN17 status and the group's fragmented aviation structure) in one transaction rather than two separate ones.

# 03: The 2026 Restructuring Journey

**Business question**: What exactly happened in January 2026, mechanically, and does the resulting entity's reported financial trend mean what it looks like it means?

--- CELL id=bccd254c ---
## The transaction, mechanically

On **26 July 2024**, AirAsia X Berhad entered a Share Sale and Purchase Agreement with Capital A Berhad to acquire 100% of AirAsia Aviation Group Limited (AAAGL), the entity holding AirAsia's short/medium-haul airlines (Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia, Philippines, Cambodia). The purchase price was satisfied entirely through shares: **2,307,692,307 new AirAsia X ordinary shares issued to Capital A at RM1.67 per share**, a total consideration of **RM3,853,846,152**. The transaction completed on **16 January 2026**. **[PUBLIC, AirAsia Group Q2 2026 quarterly announcement, Note 1b]**

Under Malaysian Financial Reporting Standard MFRS 3, this was treated as a **reverse acquisition**: although AirAsia X is the legal parent, AAAGL is treated as the accounting acquirer. This is why the consolidated financial statements issued under AirAsia X's (now AirAsia Group's) name are, for accounting purposes, a continuation of AAAGL's own historical financials, not AirAsia X's. **[PUBLIC]**

--- CELL id=73658f76 ---
## The revenue trend that isn't what it looks like

--- CELL id=78709299 ---
Revenue did not grow 6.5x between Q4 2025 and Q1 2026. What changed is which business is being reported: 2024-2025 figures are AirAsia X's standalone long-haul operation only; 2026 figures are the full consolidated group (Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia, Philippines, Cambodia, plus long-haul). Treating this as one continuous trend line, which several commentary pieces effectively do when they compare 2026 results to "last year," would produce a misleadingly dramatic growth story. Notebook 14's crisis diagnosis relies only on 2026 consolidated-to-consolidated or explicitly pro-forma comparisons, never a naive standalone-to-consolidated one.

--- CELL id=4fde72a6 ---
## Did the restructuring itself help the balance sheet?

This is a harder question to answer cleanly than it first appears, precisely because of the discontinuity above. What can be said with confidence: AirAsia X entered the merger with **positive shareholders' equity of RM519 million** (Q4 2025, its last standalone quarter), the product of its own multi-year post-restructuring recovery documented in Notebook 2. The combined AirAsia Group's shareholders' equity stood at **RM483 million** as of Q2 2026, similar in scale but now supporting a vastly larger, more complex consolidated balance sheet. Whether that represents dilution of a hard-won recovery or a reasonable trade-off for gaining a full network is a judgment call this notebook does not resolve; Notebook 14 addresses the more urgent question of near-term liquidity directly.

--- CELL id=8b48ecc9 ---
## Explain It Simply

In January 2026, AirAsia's long-haul airline (AirAsia X) formally swallowed the rest of the AirAsia network (Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia, Philippines, Cambodia) by issuing new shares to Capital A, the old parent company, in exchange for those businesses. This is why AirAsia's reported revenue appears to jump roughly 6-7x overnight starting in 2026: it isn't that the airline suddenly got much bigger by flying more passengers, it's that the accounts started reporting a much bigger combined business instead of just the long-haul piece. Any comparison of "2026 versus 2025" needs to account for this, or it will look far more dramatic, in either direction, than what actually happened operationally.

--- CELL id=650b776c ---
## Glossary

- **Reverse acquisition**: an accounting treatment (MFRS 3) where the legal acquirer is treated as the accounting acquiree, and vice versa, used when the legal subsidiary is economically the larger or more substantive business.
- **AAAGL**: AirAsia Aviation Group Limited, the entity holding AirAsia's short/medium-haul airline operations, acquired by AirAsia X in this transaction.
- **Consideration shares**: new shares issued as payment for an acquisition, instead of cash.

--- CELL id=2cd84375 ---
## Why Is This Happening?

**Why structure this as a reverse acquisition instead of a normal one?** Accounting standards require the entity that is economically dominant, here, AAAGL, the much larger short/medium-haul network, to be treated as the accounting acquirer regardless of which entity is the legal parent. This is not a choice Capital A or AirAsia X made for convenience; it follows directly from MFRS 3's rules once the deal was structured with AirAsia X as the legal issuer of shares. The practical consequence, no restated prior-year comparatives, is a direct, disclosed side effect of this rule, not a gap in reporting.

# 04: Group Structure Today: Who Owns What

**Business question**: Notebooks 1-3 traced how one company (the 1993 parent, later renamed Capital A) ended up spinning its airlines out to its own former sister company. Before this project analyses each business individually, it needs one clear, current map of who actually owns what today, since the two companies are easy to conflate given they share so much history and branding.

--- CELL id=gs-two-companies ---
## Two listed companies, not one group

As of today, **Capital A Berhad** and **AirAsia Group Berhad** are two separately listed companies on Bursa Malaysia, linked only by a minority cross-shareholding, not one consolidated group with two divisions. Confusing them is easy, since both trace back to the same 1993 company and both still use the AirAsia brand. **[PUBLIC]**

--- CELL id=gs-capitala ---
## Capital A Berhad: the original mother, now the non-aviation holding company

Capital A is the continuous legal entity founded in 1993 and bought for RM1 in 2001 (Notebook 1), renamed from AirAsia Group Berhad in January 2022 (Notebook 2). After disposing of its aviation businesses (completed December 2025-January 2026, Notebook 3), it retained five core businesses, reported as its own segments: **ADE** (aircraft maintenance, repair and overhaul), **Teleport** (air and ground logistics/cargo), **AirAsia MOVE** (the travel booking super-app, formerly airasia Superapp), **AirAsia Next** (brand licensing and loyalty, which now also houses **BigPay**, the fintech app), and **Santan** (inflight catering and food retail). For FY2025, Capital A Companies generated **RM3.39 billion in revenue**, **RM443 million EBITDA**, and **RM171 million NOP**, with a one-off gain on the aviation disposal restoring the Group to **positive shareholder equity of RM937 million**. **[PUBLIC, Capital A FY2025 financial results]**

More recently, for **2Q2026** (the same quarter used for the airline's own crisis diagnosis, Notebook 14), Capital A reported **RM809 million revenue (+9% YoY)**, **RM108 million EBITDA**, and **RM25 million Profit After Tax**, its **second consecutive profitable quarter** since the aviation disposal. On a cumulative first-half (1H2026) basis, revenue reached **RM1.58 billion (+4% YoY)**, with PAT of **RM50 million**. **[PUBLIC, Capital A Financial Results Second Quarter 2026]**

Each of these five businesses gets its own dedicated notebook (8-12), with multi-year trends and, where real data exists, a check against competitors.

--- CELL id=gs-airasiagroup ---
## AirAsia Group Berhad: legally the former sister company, now the airline holding company

The company now called AirAsia Group Berhad is, legally, the entity that used to be called AirAsia X Berhad, the long-haul affiliate founded in 2006/2007 (Notebook 1). It legally acquired Capital A's short/medium-haul airlines on 16 January 2026 (a reverse acquisition, Notebook 3) and renamed itself AirAsia Group Berhad on 2 July 2026, reusing the original parent's old name. It now consolidates six Air Operator Certificates: **Malaysia AirAsia (MAA)**, **Malaysia AirAsia X (MAAX)**, **Thai AirAsia (TAA)**, **Indonesia AirAsia (IAA)**, **Philippines AirAsia (PAA)**, and **Cambodia AirAsia (CAA)**, plus **Thai AirAsia X (TAAX)**, held as an equity-accounted associate rather than a consolidated subsidiary. For 2Q26, the consolidated group reported **RM5.09 billion in quarterly revenue**. **[PUBLIC, AirAsia Group 2Q26 Quarterly Announcement]**

This entity, and its crisis, is the subject of Notebooks 5-7 and 14 (the airline's business model, fleet, competitive position, and the September 2026 liquidity crisis).

--- CELL id=gs-crossholding ---
## The link between them: a 19.5% stake, not a parent-subsidiary relationship

Capital A is confirmed as AirAsia Group's **single largest shareholder, holding a 19.5% stake**, received as consideration for handing over its aviation businesses rather than cash. **[PUBLIC, Capital A Major Shareholders disclosure]** This means Capital A retains real economic exposure to AirAsia Group's fortunes (quantified directly in Notebook 15) without controlling it and without its results being consolidated into Capital A's own financial statements. Anyone reading a headline number for "AirAsia" without checking which of the two companies it refers to risks mixing up two different balance sheets, two different sets of subsidiaries, and two different sets of problems.

--- CELL id=gs-legal-complication ---
## A live complication in Capital A's own ownership structure

On **10 June 2026**, the Singapore High Court ordered the seizure of Move Digital Sdn Bhd's (a Capital A subsidiary) **99.56% stake in BigPay** and **11.45% stake in Teleport**, enforcing a **27 December 2024** partial arbitration award. The dispute traces back to 2021, when BigPay's minority co-founders, Christopher Davison and Navin Rajagopalan, sought a buyout of their stakes under a 2017 shareholder agreement, claiming between **US$140-183 million**; the arbitration tribunal awarded them only **US$14.736 million (approximately RM60.09 million)**. Capital A/Move Digital filed objections to the seizure, which remain pending as of this project's research. **[PUBLIC, AirAsia Group 2Q26 Quarterly Announcement, Note 33]**

This sits squarely inside Capital A's own ownership structure, not AirAsia Group's, and predates the September 2026 aviation crisis by three months. It is a genuine, independent risk factor sitting over exactly two of the five businesses (BigPay and Teleport) covered in this project's business-unit notebooks.

--- CELL id=gs-explain ---
## Explain It Simply

There isn't one "AirAsia" anymore, there are two separate public companies that both grew out of the same 1993 airline. Capital A is the original company, renamed, and it now owns the logistics arm, the aircraft maintenance business, the travel app, the loyalty and fintech brand, and the catering company, everything except the actual planes. AirAsia Group is, confusingly, the company that used to be called AirAsia X, which bought the actual airlines from Capital A in 2026 and then took over the old "AirAsia Group" name for itself. Capital A kept a 19.5% ownership slice of AirAsia Group as part of that deal, so the two are financially linked, but neither owns the other outright. On top of that, a Singapore court has ordered two of Capital A's own businesses, BigPay and part of Teleport, seized to settle an old legal dispute, a separate complication sitting inside Capital A's own house.

--- CELL id=gs-glossary ---
## Glossary

- **AOC (Air Operator Certificate)**: the regulatory licence required to operate an airline; AirAsia Group consolidates six of these.
- **Associate**: a company in which an investor holds significant influence (commonly 20-50% ownership) but not control, accounted for differently from a consolidated subsidiary; Thai AirAsia X (TAAX) is held this way.
- **Cross-shareholding**: when one company owns a minority equity stake in another, creating financial exposure without control or consolidation.
- **Arbitration award**: a binding decision from a private arbitration tribunal, enforceable through the courts like a judgment.

--- CELL id=gs-why ---
## Why Is This Happening?

**Why does this matter enough to need its own notebook, rather than letting each business-unit notebook explain the structure itself?** Every subsequent notebook in this project (5 through 15) depends on the reader already knowing which of the two companies owns which business and why the 2026 restructuring flipped the historical parent-sister relationship. Establishing it once, clearly, up front, avoids either repeating the same explanation seven times or leaving readers to infer it piecemeal, and reduces the risk of a reader misreading a segment result as belonging to the wrong parent company.

# 05: The Airline: Business Model and Unit Economics

**Business question**: Before diagnosing AirAsia's crisis, this notebook builds the vocabulary and the baseline numbers needed to read the rest of the project: how does a low-cost carrier actually make money, what do six years of the group's own revenue and profit history look like, and what do AirAsia's own real Q1-Q2 2026 numbers say about its unit economics right now?

--- CELL id=475fbb0b ---
## How a low-cost carrier makes money

Three levers matter more than anything else for an airline like AirAsia:

- **RASK (Revenue per Available Seat Kilometre)**: total revenue divided by total flying capacity. This blends ticket fares and ancillary revenue (baggage, seat selection, meals) into one number.
- **CASK (Cost per Available Seat Kilometre)**: total operating cost divided by the same capacity measure. When CASK exceeds RASK, the airline is losing money on an operating basis.
- **Load factor**: the percentage of available seats actually sold. A high load factor means the airline is filling planes efficiently; a falling one, even with strong fares, signals softer demand or a network mismatch.

--- CELL id=4b1e2b18 ---
## Six years first: the 2025-2026 fuel shock isn't the whole story

The rest of this notebook, and much of this project, zooms into the last few quarters. Before doing that, it's worth seeing the six-year backdrop those quarters sit inside, because the group's revenue base itself has changed shape more than once in that period.

--- CELL id=7373d13d ---
On a whole-group basis, the numbers tell a clean recovery story across four fiscal years: revenue collapsed from a pre-pandemic scale to **RM1.7 billion at its 2021 trough**, then recovered to **RM14.8 billion by FY2023**, a year that also delivered the group's **first full-year net profit (RM508 million) since the pandemic**. **[PUBLIC]** That took four straight years of losses totalling roughly **RM12.3 billion (FY2020-FY2022 combined)** to get there, a simple sum of the three PUBLIC annual figures above. **[DERIVED]**

The trail goes less clean after that, and the reason why matters for reading this entire project. Starting in **FY2024**, the Group began reporting its aviation business and its non-aviation businesses (Teleport, ADE, AirAsia MOVE, BigPay) as **separate segments** ahead of a planned disposal: FY2024 aviation revenue was **RM19 billion** (EBITDA RM3.2 billion), while non-aviation revenue was **just under RM3.0 billion** (profit after tax RM147 million), but the press release does not state a single consolidated net profit or loss for the year on a comparable basis to FY2020-FY2023. By **FY2025**, the aviation business had been **disposed to AirAsia X Berhad, completing on 3 December 2025**; what remained as "Capital A" for that year was the non-aviation businesses alone, with revenue of **RM3.39 billion** and a one-off gain on the disposal that restored the Group to **positive shareholder equity of RM937 million**. **[PUBLIC]**

That is not a data gap in this project; it is the same entity discontinuity documented in **Notebook 3**, visible a year earlier in the raw financial reporting than in the January 2026 reverse acquisition itself. The group whose unit economics this notebook analyses for 2025-2026 is, legally, a different entity (AirAsia X Berhad, renamed AirAsia Group Berhad) from the one that reported RM14.8 billion in FY2023 revenue. The six-year revenue and profit trend above is real and instructive, but it should be read as the story of the underlying flying business and its economics, not as a single continuous company's income statement.

--- CELL id=cfffd4d2 ---
## AirAsia's own real numbers, Q1-Q2 2026

--- CELL id=71953cc6 ---
This is the clearest single chart in this project. In 1Q26 and both pro-forma comparatives, RASK sat comfortably above CASK, a healthy operating margin per seat kilometre. In 2Q26, CASK jumped to 22.72 sen against RASK of 21.28 sen, an operating loss of roughly 1.44 sen on every available seat kilometre flown. **[PUBLIC, AirAsia Group 1Q26 and 2Q26 investor presentations]**

The right-hand chart shows why: average fuel price jumped from **$110/barrel in 1Q26 to $183/barrel in 2Q26**, a 66% jump within the same year, on top of already being higher than the $95-101/barrel range in the prior-year quarters. This is the same 2026 global oil price shock (the Iran war and Strait of Hormuz disruption) that shows up across commodity markets generally, landing directly on AirAsia's cost base. Note that CASK ex-fuel actually **fell** to 11.02 sen in 2Q26 (down 7% YoY), meaning the airline's non-fuel cost discipline improved; the loss is fuel-driven, not a broader cost-control failure.

--- CELL id=9f56f831 ---
## Load factor: softening, but not collapsing

Load factor fell from 86% (1Q25 proforma) to 85% (1Q26), and from 82% (2Q25 proforma) to 80% (2Q26). A 1-2 percentage point year-on-year decline is a real softening signal, worth watching, but it is not the kind of double-digit collapse that would by itself explain a solvency crisis. **[PUBLIC]** Combined with the fuel evidence above, the picture this notebook supports is: demand is softening modestly, but the dominant driver of 2Q26's operating pressure is the external fuel price shock, not a demand collapse.

--- CELL id=1748c746 ---
## Why is load factor softening: fare choice, not falling demand

The company's own 2Q26 investor presentation gives a direct answer this notebook's earlier draft didn't have: **3-month forward bookings were holding at the same level as the prior year, even with fares up 21%**, and **70-80% of bookings continued to be made within 90 days of departure**, which the company reads as customers adapting to the new dynamic fuel-surcharge pricing rather than avoiding AirAsia altogether. **[PUBLIC, AirAsia Group 2Q26 investor presentation]**

Domestic Malaysia is the clearest evidence for this: revenue there fell only 1% year-on-year despite a 13% capacity cut, because a 24-29% fare increase and 19% RASK growth more than offset fewer seats sold. Reading this alongside the softening load factor above, the picture is not "fewer people want to fly AirAsia." It is closer to "AirAsia raised fares hard enough, in response to the fuel shock, that a couple of load-factor points were traded away for meaningfully higher revenue per seat," a deliberate yield-over-volume choice rather than a demand collapse. **[PUBLIC]**

This project cannot independently verify forward-booking data since it isn't published outside the company's own disclosures, so this reading rests on AirAsia's own claim rather than an outside data source (a ticket-price scrape or travel-agent survey, for instance). It is included because it is the most direct evidence available on the demand question, not because it has been cross-checked against an independent source.

--- CELL id=e805a9ad ---
## Explain It Simply

Zooming out first: it took AirAsia four straight years of losses, roughly RM12.3 billion combined, to climb back from COVID's RM1.7 billion revenue trough in 2021 to its first profitable full year in 2023. After that, the group's own accounting got more complicated, not because of hidden problems, but because it started splitting itself into pieces (aviation versus the digital/logistics businesses) ahead of eventually selling the aviation piece to its own sister company in 2026, which is the reverse acquisition covered in Notebook 3.

Zooming into the last year specifically: airlines make money by selling more revenue per seat-kilometre flown (RASK) than it costs them to fly that seat-kilometre (CASK). For most of the last year, AirAsia managed this fine. Then in the second quarter of 2026, the price of jet fuel nearly doubled because of a global oil crisis, and suddenly the cost side outran the revenue side. The airline actually got better at controlling its non-fuel costs over the same period, cutting them by 7%, so this isn't a story of AirAsia losing its grip on spending. It's a story of an external oil shock hitting at a bad time, layered on top of a company still only a few years removed from a genuine near-death experience.

--- CELL id=c51112bd ---
## Glossary

- **RASK / CASK**: Revenue and Cost per Available Seat Kilometre, the standard airline unit-economics measures.
- **ASK**: Available Seat Kilometres, total flying capacity (seats multiplied by distance flown).
- **Load factor**: the share of available seats actually sold.
- **Pro-forma**: a restated figure showing what results would have looked like under a different structure, here, as if the January 2026 consolidation had applied a year earlier, for a fairer year-on-year comparison.
- **PAT (Profit After Tax)** and **NOP (Net Operating Profit)**: two different bottom-line measures Capital A has used across its FY2020-FY2025 reporting; NOP is a Capital A-specific operating profit measure that excludes certain one-off items, which is why it is not always directly comparable to the plain net profit/loss figures used in earlier years.

--- CELL id=4cf8941e ---
## Why Is This Happening?

**Why does a fuel price swing hit a low-cost carrier harder than a full-service one?** Low-cost carriers run thinner margins per seat by design, competing on price, so they have less cushion to absorb a cost shock before it turns an operating profit into an operating loss. AirAsia's own data shows this precisely: CASK ex-fuel actually improved, but the fuel line alone was enough to flip the total unit-cost picture. A full-service carrier with higher RASK to begin with (from business/first class revenue, cargo, and alliance revenue-sharing) has more room to absorb the same fuel shock before crossing into a loss.

# 06: The Airline: Fleet and Capacity Rationalization

**Business question**: Early research for this project turned up a claim that roughly 30% of AirAsia's fleet was grounded for "rationalization." That figure is checked directly against the company's own disclosures here, since it materially changes how severe the capacity story looks.

--- CELL id=7e305f82 ---
## Correcting an earlier assumption

Direct extraction from AirAsia Group's own 2Q26 investor presentation shows the real figure is far smaller than the 30% initially assumed: the fleet went from 245 aircraft (2Q25) to 240 (1Q26) to 239 (2Q26), a reduction of only **6 aircraft, about 2.4%**. **[PUBLIC]**

--- CELL id=11f28cf8 ---
## What is actually happening to the fleet

The company's own "Fleet Rightsizing" plan for 2026 is to return a total of **25 older aircraft** across the year, including **17 early returns** (ahead of their scheduled lease end dates), while receiving **2 new A321LR aircraft** (delivered April and June 2026) and expecting **3 more in 2027**. Separately, as of 2Q26: 1 aircraft was reactivated, 5 aircraft were undergoing checks (targeted to return to service 3Q-4Q26), and 1 widebody aircraft remained in storage for engine replacement. **[PUBLIC, AirAsia Group 2Q26 investor presentation]**

So the real story is a **gradual, planned fleet renewal and rightsizing programme**, returning older, higher-maintenance aircraft on favourable lease terms while phasing in newer, more fuel-efficient aircraft, not an emergency grounding of a third of the fleet. The 11% year-on-year ASK (capacity) cut identified in Notebook 5 comes more from reduced utilisation and route/frequency discipline ("held every route to strict profitability hurdle-rates," in the company's own words) than from a dramatically smaller fleet.

--- CELL id=0076a20e ---
## A discrepancy inside the company's own disclosures, disclosed rather than hidden

Checking this more closely surfaced something worth flagging plainly: AirAsia Group's own 2Q26 **quarterly report narrative** (Note 27, "Review of Group Performance") states "approximately 30% of the Group's fleet (161 operating aircraft as at the end of the quarter) was non-operational." That figure does not appear anywhere else in the same company's own disclosures for the same quarter. The quarterly report's own operating statistics table lists 239 total aircraft with no operating/non-operating split. The separate 2Q26 **investor presentation** gives a specific breakdown instead: an "Active Fleet" of **231 of 239 aircraft (96.7%)**, with the gap accounted for by the 1 reactivated, 5 under checks, and 1 in storage detailed above, roughly **8 aircraft, about 3.3%**, not 30%. **[PUBLIC, both documents dated the same reporting period]**

This project relies on the investor presentation's figure, since it is the one backed by an itemised breakdown that adds up, rather than an unreconciled round number in the quarterly report's prose. But the honest position is that AirAsia's own disclosures contain two different answers to "what share of the fleet isn't flying," a full order of magnitude apart, for the same quarter, and this project cannot independently resolve which one the company actually meant. Readers checking the primary sources themselves should expect to find both numbers and should not assume this project picked the more flattering one without saying so.

--- CELL id=b2b70f94 ---
## The capacity cuts are mostly about routes, not just aircraft

The 11% year-on-year ASK cut is not evenly spread. AirAsia Indonesia (IAA) and AirAsia Philippines (PAA), the two units the company itself flags as loss-making, had **33 unviable routes permanently cut**, bringing total permanent route closures to **64 for the Group year-to-date June 2026**. A further **17 routes were temporarily paused** across 2Q/3Q, with scheduling flexibility to restore them in late 2026 if conditions improve. Separately, the planned **Bahrain hub and a new London route were delayed to next year** to preserve capital. **[PUBLIC, AirAsia Group 2Q26 investor presentation]**

Guidance for the rest of the year cuts both ways: management is guiding capacity down a further **20-25% year-on-year for 3Q26** (the seasonally leanest quarter), before an explicit plan to **ramp capacity back up in 4Q26** toward pre-shock levels to capture year-end holiday demand. This is not a company retreating uniformly; it is concentrating cuts on specific underperforming markets and the leanest quarter, while planning to re-expand into the strongest one.

--- CELL id=de23332c ---
## Is this smart cost-cutting or shrinking the way out?

Returning older aircraft on favourable lease terms while bringing in more efficient replacements is, on its face, a reasonable response to a fuel price shock (Notebook 5): the aircraft being removed are disproportionately the ones with the worst fuel economy. The risk is that disciplined capacity cuts, if they persist too long or go too far, reduce the revenue base available to earn out of the working capital deficit identified in Notebook 14. Nothing in the disclosed plan suggests the cuts are currently at that scale; this is a real risk to monitor going forward, not a finding this notebook can confirm has already happened.

--- CELL id=86ab0b6c ---
## Explain It Simply

An earlier piece of research for this project suggested AirAsia had grounded about a third of its planes, which would be a genuinely alarming sign of a company in freefall. Checking the actual company disclosures directly shows that claim mostly doesn't hold up: the fleet shrank by only 6 aircraft, about 2%, and the company's own investor presentation says only about 8 aircraft (3%) weren't flying at quarter-end. Oddly, the same company's own quarterly report text separately claims 30% wasn't flying, a number that doesn't match anything else the company disclosed for the same period; we're flagging that contradiction rather than quietly picking whichever number sounds better. What is really happening, and better documented, is a planned programme to retire older, less fuel-efficient planes early (25 of them across 2026) while bringing in newer ones, combined with cutting 64 underperforming routes (mostly in Indonesia and the Philippines) and guiding capacity down another 20-25% for the next quarter before ramping back up for year-end holidays. That's disciplined route and fleet management responding to expensive fuel, not a sign of a company shutting itself down.

--- CELL id=dc28cbfb ---
## Glossary

- **Fleet rightsizing**: matching the number and type of aircraft to actual demand and route economics, often by returning leased aircraft early.
- **ASK (Available Seat Kilometres)**: total flying capacity, driven by both fleet size and how intensively that fleet is utilised.
- **IAA / PAA**: AirAsia Indonesia and AirAsia Philippines, the two country units the company itself identifies as loss-making and where most permanent route closures were concentrated.

--- CELL id=e49458bf ---
## Why Is This Happening?

**Why return older aircraft specifically, rather than cutting capacity evenly across the fleet?** Older aircraft carry worse fuel economy and higher maintenance costs, precisely the two expense lines under the most pressure from the 2026 fuel shock (Notebook 5). Returning them early and replacing with newer aircraft (A321LR, and A220/A321XLR expected from 2028) is a way to cut the cost side of the unit-economics equation structurally, rather than simply flying fewer of the same planes. This is a genuinely different strategy from an across-the-board capacity freeze, and a more defensible one if the fuel shock persists.

# 08: Teleport (Logistics)

**Business question**: Teleport is Capital A's single largest revenue contributor and the business most directly caught in a legal dispute over its own shares (Notebook 4). Is it actually a good business on its own merits, and how does it stack up against dedicated logistics competitors?

--- CELL id=tp-model ---
## The business model: asset-light, capacity-blended

Teleport moves cargo and e-commerce parcels by combining three capacity sources: **AirAsia Group's own belly space** (the cargo hold beneath passenger flights), **third-party partner airline capacity** (over 30 airline partnerships), and **its own freighter aircraft** (three Airbus A321 Freighters as of FY2023, since expanded). This asset-light structure means Teleport's growth is not capped by how many freighters it owns outright. **[PUBLIC, Capital A financial results press releases]**

--- CELL id=tp-trend ---
## Three years of real trend: fast growth, then a real turnaround

Revenue grew from **RM731 million (FY2023)** to **RM1.1 billion (FY2024, +49% YoY)** to **RM1.2 billion (FY2025, +11% YoY)**, a genuine deceleration in growth rate as the base got larger, not a red flag on its own. The more interesting story is the bottom line: FY2024 closed with a **RM21 million net loss** despite EBITDA growing to RM90 million that year (interest and depreciation on the growing freighter fleet ate the difference), before Teleport achieved a **RM18.6 million Net Operating Profit in FY2025**, a genuine RM40 million swing, driven partly by refinancing its Deutsche Bank loan in 3Q2025 to lower finance costs. **[PUBLIC, Capital A FY2023-FY2025 financial results press releases]**

Volume tells the same growth story from the operating side: FY2025 total volume reached **347,885 tonnes (+18% YoY)** and **167 million parcels moved (+99% YoY)**, with the parcel count roughly doubling even as tonnage grew more modestly, a shift toward higher-frequency, smaller e-commerce shipments over bulk cargo. **[PUBLIC]**

Within Capital A's own portfolio, Teleport is the anchor: it and ADE together generated over 70% of 1H26 group revenue, with Teleport alone about 40% of Capital A's Q1 2026 revenue (Notebook 4).

--- CELL id=6e9ce4d4 ---
## The turnaround is continuing into 2026, not a one-off

The FY2025 turnaround was not a one-time event. For **2Q2026**, Teleport reported **revenue of RM311 million (+22% YoY)**, **EBITDA of RM26.8 million (+9% YoY)**, its **strongest 2Q EBITDA to date**, and **PAT of RM4.4 million**, its **fourth consecutive profitable quarter**. On a first-half (1H2026) basis, revenue reached **RM620 million (+21% YoY)**, and PAT turned to **+RM12.2 million**, against an actual **RM1.5 million loss in 1H2025**, a genuine swing back to profitability rather than a single strong quarter. **[PUBLIC, Capital A Financial Results Second Quarter 2026]**

--- CELL id=tp-competitor ---
## How this compares: a real competitor, but a very different scale

**J&T Express**, a publicly-reporting regional logistics company, is a legitimate comparison since both move parcels across Southeast Asia, though its core model (ground-based parcel delivery network) differs structurally from Teleport's air-cargo, belly-space-blended model. For FY2025, J&T Express's Southeast Asia segment alone reported **US$4.5 billion in revenue (+39.8% YoY)** on **7.66 billion parcels (+67.8% YoY)**. **[PUBLIC, J&T Express FY2025 results]**

At roughly RM1.2 billion (about USD260-280 million depending on the exchange rate used), Teleport's revenue is on the order of **15-17 times smaller** than J&T's Southeast Asia segment alone, and its 167 million annual parcels are a small fraction of J&T's 7.66 billion in the same region. This is not a fair apples-to-apples read on execution quality, since the business models differ (air cargo and belly-space brokering versus a dedicated ground parcel network), but it is an honest scale check: Teleport is a genuinely fast-growing niche player within a market where a dedicated regional logistics operator is more than an order of magnitude larger. **[DERIVED, this project's own comparison]**

--- CELL id=tp-explain ---
## Explain It Simply

Teleport is Capital A's cargo and delivery business, and it doesn't own a huge fleet of its own, it mostly borrows space in the belly of AirAsia's passenger planes, buys capacity from other airlines, and only owns a few dedicated freighter planes. Its revenue nearly doubled over two years, but in 2024 it actually lost money on the bottom line even while its core operating profit grew, mostly due to interest costs on debt. In 2025 it fixed that, refinanced its debt, and turned an actual profit for the first time. Compared to J&T Express, a real logistics company operating across the same region, Teleport is still 15-20 times smaller, so while it's Capital A's star performer internally, it's a small player in the wider regional logistics market.

--- CELL id=tp-glossary ---
## Glossary

- **Belly space**: the cargo hold underneath a passenger aircraft's main deck, used to carry freight alongside passengers.
- **Freighter aircraft**: an aircraft configured to carry cargo only, with no passenger seating.
- **NOP (Net Operating Profit)**: a Capital A-specific measure of core operating profit, excluding certain one-off items.

--- CELL id=tp-why ---
## Why Is This Happening?

**Why did EBITDA grow strongly in FY2024 while the bottom line still showed a loss?** EBITDA excludes interest and depreciation, the exact costs that scale up when a business finances growth (new freighters, expanded operations) with debt. Teleport's FY2024 pattern, rising EBITDA alongside a net loss, is a common signature of a business scaling capital-intensive infrastructure faster than it can pay down the associated financing costs. The FY2025 turnaround came specifically from addressing the financing side (refinancing the Deutsche Bank loan) rather than from a fundamental change in the underlying operating business, which was already healthy in FY2024.

**Business question**: On 17 September 2026, AirAsia and Capital A shares fell 21% and 18% after reports the Malaysian government was in contingency talks with Malaysia Airlines and Batik Air about absorbing AirAsia's domestic routes. --- CELL id=2ac63770 ---
## Reconciling the disputed number

--- CELL id=d20c6a1e ---
Both framings check out on their own narrow terms, and both are incomplete. The panic headline's RM18.4 billion is real: it is exactly the reported total current liabilities as of 30 June 2026, directly from AirAsia Group's filed balance sheet. **[PUBLIC]** Fernandes's rebuttal is also real: current lease liabilities are exactly RM2.76 billion, about 15% of that total. **[PUBLIC]**

But neither number mentioned is the one a credit analyst would actually reach for first: **current assets of only RM3.87 billion against RM18.41 billion of current liabilities**, a working capital deficit of **RM14.5 billion**. Roughly RM10.26 billion of the current liabilities is trade and other payables (money owed to suppliers, lessors, and vendors), a genuine, if less headline-grabbing, near-term obligation. A further RM2.09 billion is sales in advance (tickets already sold for future flights), which is not really distress risk at all; it converts to flown revenue rather than a cash outflow. Cash on hand is **RM954 million**. **[PUBLIC]**

--- CELL id=c2a97d00 ---
## The honest diagnosis

The specific claim that spooked the market, "RM18.4 billion in liabilities" read as if it were all urgent near-term debt, was imprecise; a large share of it is ordinary trade payables and deferred ticket revenue, not distress-specific debt. The CEO's rebuttal correctly identifies this. But his response, framed narrowly around lease liabilities, does not address the underlying working-capital deficit, which is real, large, and is the metric that actually determines whether a company can meet its near-term obligations from what it currently holds. A RM14.5 billion shortfall against RM954 million in cash is a genuine liquidity stress signal, independent of how the RM18.4 billion gets sliced.

This connects directly to Notebook 5's finding: the 2Q26 operating loss (CASK exceeding RASK) was driven by the fuel price shock, not a broad cost-control failure. That supports treating this as a real but externally-triggered stress event rather than evidence of mismanagement, while still being a serious liquidity position that explains why the government is doing contingency planning.

--- CELL id=a6e3a6bb ---
## How urgent is it actually: a real burn-rate check

A working capital deficit is a snapshot. It doesn't say whether the gap is a three-week problem or a nine-month one. AirAsia Group's own filed cash flow statement makes that checkable directly.

--- CELL id=469b68b5 ---
Two things are true at once, and both matter for how urgent this actually is. First, on a pure operating-cash-burn basis, RM954 million in cash buys roughly **9-10 months** of runway if nothing else changed, meaningfully longer than the "weeks away from trouble" tone of the September panic coverage, though this is a rough average across a single half-year, not a forecast, and a second consecutive quarter like 2Q26 (higher fuel, a net loss) would burn faster than 1Q26 did. **[DERIVED, from the Group's own filed 1H2026 cash flow statement]**

Second, and more important: AirAsia Group is not sitting still against a fixed cash pile. In the same six months it raised **RM2.81 billion** in new financing (a private placement, new debentures, and new borrowings) while repaying **RM2.40 billion** of existing borrowings, debentures, and lease obligations, a net financing inflow of about **RM404 million**. The company is already actively rolling a large amount of debt in real time. The **USD1 billion plus RM700 million** it is separately seeking (Notebook 17) is incremental to this already-substantial ongoing activity, not a first attempt at addressing the gap. **[PUBLIC]**

--- CELL id=0ab5c0cb ---
Worth noting for urgency: the Group's most recent completed capital raise, a RM1.0 billion private placement announced back in July 2024, has already been **100% utilised** as of this report, split between working capital for the enlarged aviation group (RM954.6 million) and deal expenses (RM45.4 million). There is no unspent buffer sitting from that earlier raise; the new financing being sought now would be genuinely new money, not a top-up of something already in the bank. **[PUBLIC, AirAsia Group 2Q26 Quarterly Announcement, Note 32]**

## Explain It Simply

When news broke that the government was quietly planning for other airlines to take over AirAsia's routes, everyone pointed to AirAsia owing RM18.4 billion as proof of a crisis. AirAsia's CEO pushed back, saying most of that isn't real debt, only RM2.8 billion of it is loans on planes. Both of them are technically right and both are leaving something out. The RM18.4 billion is real, but a lot of it is normal business bills and pre-sold tickets, not emergency debt. But focusing only on the lease liabilities misses the bigger, plainer problem: AirAsia only has RM3.9 billion in cash and short-term assets to cover RM18.4 billion in short-term bills, a RM14.5 billion gap, with less than RM1 billion in the bank. That gap is the real number to worry about, and neither the panic headline nor the CEO's rebuttal said it plainly.

--- CELL id=4a279868 ---
## Glossary

- **Working capital deficit**: when current liabilities (due within 12 months) exceed current assets, a standard signal of near-term liquidity stress.
- **Trade and other payables**: amounts owed to suppliers, vendors, and lessors for goods/services already received.
- **Sales in advance**: cash already collected for tickets on flights not yet flown, a liability on the books but not a distress signal in the way debt is.
- **Cash burn rate**: the pace at which a company's cash balance is depleted by ongoing operations, usually expressed per month; tells you how much runway a fixed cash balance actually buys.

--- CELL id=07fb8c55 ---
## Why Is This Happening?

**Why would a company with a genuine RM14.5 billion working capital deficit still be operating normally, still flying, still selling tickets?** Airlines routinely run negative working capital as a structural feature of the business, since they collect cash for tickets well before they incur the cost of flying the passenger (the RM2.09 billion in sales in advance is part of this). A working capital deficit is a red flag when it is large and growing without offsetting cash generation; it is closer to normal, if uncomfortable, in an industry where prepaid revenue is standard. What makes this instance worth the government's attention is the combination of the deficit's scale (RM14.5 billion against under RM1 billion cash) with an operating loss in the same quarter (Notebook 5), removing the cushion that ordinarily lets airlines carry negative working capital indefinitely.

A fair synthesis has to hold both at once, past institutional resilience exists, but it belongs to AirAsia X specifically, not yet to the untested combined AirAsia Group, rather than quietly using only the flattering half.

**The government's lighter-touch response is genuinely ambiguous, and this project should not resolve that ambiguity by omission.** Notebook 16 explicitly presented two readings, a milder assessment of severity, or simply the absence of an ownership lever like the one Khazanah held over MAS in 2014, and stated it does not know which explains the lighter response. Treating the lighter-touch response as evidence of lower severity, without carrying forward that same hedge, overstates what Notebook 16 actually supports.

**What can honestly be said**: the RM14.5 billion working capital deficit (Notebook 14) is real and does not depend on any of the above being resolved either way. The fuel-driven, not-mismanagement read of the 2Q26 loss (Notebook 5) is comparatively solid, since it rests on the company's own disclosed CASK ex-fuel improvement, not on an interpretation of ambiguous signals. The AirAsia X precedent (Notebook 2) and the government's posture (Notebook 16) are both real data points, but neither is a strong reason for optimism on its own once their own stated ambiguity is taken seriously; they are pieces of context, not evidence that resolves the outcome.

## Explain It Simply

Nobody, including this project, knows for certain what happens next to AirAsia. What can be said is that two things mostly decide it: whether AirAsia can raise the money it's trying to raise, and whether jet fuel prices come back down. If both go AirAsia's way, this crisis probably fades quietly. If neither does, the more severe scenarios discussed in the news, like other airlines taking over its routes, become more realistic.

An earlier version of this notebook argued the overall balance of evidence leaned toward the less alarming outcome, using AirAsia X's past recovery and the government's calm-seeming response as support. On a closer look, that argument was too generous to itself: this same project's own Notebook 7 explains AirAsia's current underperformance by pointing to exactly the thing Notebook 2 used as a reason for confidence, that the merged company is brand new. And Notebook 16 said plainly it couldn't tell whether the government's light touch means low risk or simply that it has no ownership stake to leverage. The one finding that doesn't depend on picking a favorable interpretation is that the cash crunch is real and the loss is fuel-driven rather than self-inflicted. Beyond that, this project genuinely does not have strong enough evidence to lean toward either the calm or the alarming outcome, and says so plainly rather than picking a side to sound more conclusive.
